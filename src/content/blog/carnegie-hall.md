So, how do you get to Carnegie Hall? Practice. That's how the joke goes. I never got to Carnegie Hall but I did practice. There's a whole conversation to be had about talent and nature vs. nurture and all that. I wouldn't say I was talented. I did grow up with musical parents. My mom, a cellist, would bring us little kids to her orchestra rehearsals quite often. Mostly because we didn't have money for baby sitters or nannies or anything like that. 

This happened both in Latvia and the US. I distinctly remember sitting on a bass stool in the pit of the [Riga Operetta Theatre](https://operetesteatris.lv/en/) and watching rehearsals with my brother. It was interesting, it was boring, it was all the things. I was maybe 6 years old at the time and didn't or couldn't appreciate what was happening. The same would happen later when we moved to the US. She'd have orchestra rehearsal at night and we were too young to leave home alone. We'd take our homework and sit in the hall, red velvet seats all around, and listen to the rehearsal in the background. I remember it was quite impressive when they were rehearsing a piece that got really loud. It was also cool when they played something I recognised from the TV or radio.

I myself started music when I was quite young in Latvia. I'd go to my piano lessons, cry, come home, and practice for 5 minutes. I really liked playing the piano, but I didn't like playing what my teacher wanted me to play. I enjoyed making up my own melodies and that was enough for me. I started to play viola when I was in elementary school. It was smaller than my mom's cello, but bigger than the violin which is what all the girls chose. That's 9-year-old logic right there.

Anyway, to the practising thing. As I said, I don't think I was talented, but I was persistent. With this persistence, I was able to get quite far. I played chamber music with some really great musicians at school. I got to play in orchestras under some really great conductors like <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Robertson_(conductor)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">David Robertson </a>, <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Oundjian" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Peter Oundjian</a>, <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paavo_J%C3%A4rvi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Paavo Jarvi</a>, and others. I got to travel and I got to meet my wife. But all of this was from putting in the time. Hours every day, playing scales, arpeggios, my etudes and studies, orchestral excerpts, and whatever else I needed. 

There was articel about this topic as it relates to software development by Addy Osmani: [Agentic Skill Decay](https://addyo.substack.com/p/agentic-skill-decay). He mentions that in the current software climate, you need to deliberate about creating an environment where you're still exercising your brain muscles.

Here are a couple of quotes: 

- "Mastery still comes from doing the reps."
- "Now, I think that for many people who are getting started with AI, you can short-circuit a lot of the learning journey. You can go very quickly from, hey, here’s a problem, to, well, hey, here’s the solution, or here’s the outcome of the overall task, while skipping all of those things that would otherwise have built up your knowledge base, or helped educate you about, you know, don’t do that thing, this is why you don’t do that thing, do this thing, and help you reason about the trade-offs."

I think that second one is really the key here. Yes, we can get to a solution quickly but we might not know why or really how it came about, why it works, if there are any solutions that might fit better, etc. You could argue, and I probably would also, that this kind of shortcut development has always been the case. The 'I just copied if from Stack Overflow' type of development isn't anything new even in the SO era. And in that case, it's always been (and maybe always will be), that thing that separates engineers. There are those that are curious, want to put in the time, want to put in the practice to get better, and then there are those who just want to get it done. Sometimes we need to flip between the two depending on the circumstances, but I think I consciously try to be the former.

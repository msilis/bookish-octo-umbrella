There's something I need to do at work. I'm working on adding a new feature and it'll get pretty complex. What am I saying 'no thank you' to? Getting Claude to just pump it out and just be part of the clean-up crew afterwards. In the end it will probably be a combination effort, but I want to talk about why I don't want to just have it generate everything. I'll also link to a couple of articles I read today, one which got me pretty down, and the other which was good information.

"LLMs amplify good engineering, they also amplify bad engineering."

I have no idea if that is an actual quote, I can't be bothered to Google (or DuckDuckGo) and research who said what when. But regardless, I think it's true. I mean, I think we all can agree it's true. Or you can disagree and email me about it. Back to this feature I'm working on; I've spent some time in conversation with Claude Code about the ins and outs of what has to happen. I come back every now and then with a question or an observation and we update the plan. I've been referring to the plan for a few days now to help create a mental picture of the changes that will need to be made. But I almost feel it in my bones that I don't want it to even do the first pass. Why? Because I want to bash my head against a brick wall actually. Well, sort of. I want to have that friction that will hopefully mean that I not only have a better understanding of how this feature is pulled in, but the details of what it can do, where it can be extended, and what the limitations are.

Maybe if I'd been in the industry for decades, or maybe just a decade, I'd have a different view having gone through the growing and teething pains of trying to get something new to work. But, I don't have that luxury. There is a dissonance between the two main things you see online: don't read the code and let the LLM loop until it gets you the result you want vs. know what good engineering is and use that to guide you in your decisions. Well, how do you get the latter without getting into the weeds? Where exactly is this knowledge supposed to come from? Wait, didn't I just write about something similar like a week ago? Never mind that. Brian Jenney posts about engineering practices with LLMs and his most recent post had some good suggestions about how you can or should use LLMs to grow your knowledge. You can read it here:<a href="https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/worst-ai-career-path-meat-proxy-brian-jenney-fgyhc/" rel="noopener noreferrer">The WORST AI Career Path: Meat Proxy</a>. Sure, I've been a meat proxy myself. And I might be a bit of one when I implement this new feature, but it's with more intention (I think) than what Brian is talking about. One of the use cases I've found my friend Claude most helpful with is having those exploratory conversations. You're not really bouncing ideas off of each other in the traditional sense (since it doesn't really have actual ideas), but you can hash out a pretty good picture of the work to be done. 

If you were a developer 10 years ago, or any time pre-LLMs, sure, you would have started as a meat proxy. You would have tried something out you found on Stack Overflow, maybe it worked without you needing to make changes, maybe it didn't. But all of those little interactions built up your muscle memory. Muscle memory that came in handy when the next decision needed to be made. You would have gotten to a point where you were less of a meat proxy because you had a good understanding of how the systems you were responsible for worked. In our current situation, getting that kind of muscle memory is so much harder. 

There was another post that I read this morning (I really have to stop doing this to myself) that tinted the day a little darker. This was Henry Stanley's <a href="https://henryaj.substack.com/p/the-loop-closes">The Loop Closes</a>. This I guess is on the opposite end of the spectrum from meat proxy, and you probably would be asking if that muscle memory I was talking about earlier even matters. I don't know much about Stanley but from my quick search, he's got credentials to back up what he's talking about. The one comment (at the time I read it) asked what his emotional reaction to this was -- (side-note: how the hell do you do an em-dash in markdown... or on a keyboard in general?!) his position that humans would at some point not even be necessary to be 'in the loop'. He answered that he was excited mostly, and a couple of other things. I'd be curious to know what the exciting part is about leaving everything to the machines. He also mentions that we're moving in that direction because LLMs have gotten so much better at a lot of the work humans were responsible for when it came to engineering, including judging whether something was good or not (his reference to one of his other posts talking about how these models now have taste). Hm, frontier models have taste? I don't know. I mean, I don't know enough about the nuts and bolts of how their training and subsequent reinforcement learning might get you taste but I'm sceptical. It can apply rules, weigh evidence, but in the end it's running a mathematical prediction of what it thinks you want. 

This current timeline really is a roller-coaster ride. Ups, downs, side-quests, who knows what else. 

Back to the work that I started this with. Even though I could let my buddy Claude loop through and get most of the job done. *I* want to know. *I* want to have the experience because *I* want to have a better understanding. I'll let you know how it goes.

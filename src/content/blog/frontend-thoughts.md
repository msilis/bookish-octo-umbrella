There was an [article](https://nolanlawson.com/2026/08/23/the-asteroid-currently-hitting-frontend-web-development/)<a href="https://nolanlawson.com/2026/08/23/the-asteroid-currently-hitting-frontend-web-development/" target="blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"></a> in the recent [Frontend Focus](https://frontendfoc.us/) newsletter that talked about the question of where the frontend discipline is headed in the world of AI. It was an interesting read that felt, even by the author's opinion, gloomy. 

I was thinking about how this applies to my own job at the moment and whether I'm seeing any of the issues he brought up. I sometimes use Claude to talk through implementation of a feature we're trying to add and then I go and make the changes. I don't do a 'point and shoot' method or tell it to set off a swarm of agents to deal with all of the aspects of the software development lifecycle. For some people this would signal that I'm doing things the 'old' way and avoiding progress. But really, in the projects I work on (mostly being legacy and large codebases) it isn't that great at making things happen. I also have knowledge of the reasons behind many of the code changes we've made in the almost three years I've been working in the codebase.

So, for me, getting better at my discipline still matters. It's true that web changes are lower risk than, as the author points out, database migrations or complex back-end operations. I can also deploy a site without needing to maintain backwards compatibility or worrying about clients not being able to use the new features. So, sure, maybe an LLM could just get a lot of this done, but there's another article that also comes to mind: [AI Coding Will Prevent Expertise](https://larsfaye.com/articles/ai-coding-will-prevent-expertise). There's one section that I've seen come up in other articles as well: 'The friction is a feature'. Getting stuck into a codebase, gettng lost, finding your way out, trying a solution only to find that it doesn't work, but maybe does if it's moved to a different layer, ...that all might seem like a waste of time. But where is the learning supposed to happen? We're not in a place where we can load knowledge in our brains Matrix-style. 

I think that's one of the important questions about the preferred workflow (agentic, loop, graph, whatever it will be next week) the a lot of the internet advocates. If you're starting out or are early in your career and have done most of your learning in the post-LLM era, how are you supposed to get the gritty, dirty, knowledge required to be an effective engineer without going through the friction of doing some of this stuff without just setting an agent at it?

You may have your own views on this and that's fine, everyone should. You need to find what works for you.

There is the saying that \_practice makes perfect\_ and it always bugged me especially as a music teacher. As teachers we (my wife and I, and others we worked with/were taught by) always said "No, practice makes permanent". I still have a chip on my shoulder about the word \_perfect\_, but maybe that's another story.

I was thinking about this when practising violin with my own son. When he practices by himself, he's like I used to be, maybe like most people are. He plays through pieces without thinking too much about it and then considers the piece 'practices'. When I was going through my own early music education, I had no idea how to practice or what that actually meant. 

So, to his great annoyance, I'm his constant reminder to just repeat a small chunk. Slow down. Can you play these four notes in tune? Can you play the next four in tune? Can you put those two chunks together and play all eight in tune? Practice is repetition. You're trying to get to a state where when you play a passage you pretty much know the mechanics of what you need to do without thinking too much about it. What do you think about? The musicality of what you're playing. Phrasing, like in language, is what makes music interesting and provides the listener with your interpretation of what the composer wrote. 

Does this have anything to do with software? My answer is maybe? 

There are two scenarios that I think could play out. Scenario 1: LLMs get more and more powerful, get larger context windows, hallucinate less, and through harnesses, maybe in some way become more predictable and can be used reliably to just build thinks with natural language. Scenario 2: they don't.

Sure it's not as black and white as this, but hey, I don't feel like typing all that out.

Even if scenario 1 is possible, there is time before it probably happens on a meaningful scale, we need people to be able to build stuff. Where do you get the practice of putting these systems together if you're chucking everything at an LLM? Where do you get the <a href="https://gr-y.uk/ais-frictionless-road-to-hell/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">friction</a> of learning and growing? There's a reason you can't just watch a bunch of YouTube videos on, say, electrical repair and then expect to just be able to do stuff Matrix-style.

My approach, which I'd say works pretty well before 3PM, is to sit in whatever competence discomfort I have and read, experiment, re-evaluate, before calling in the robots. Or sometimes I don't call them in, or maybe I just plan. But my goal is to get better at the thing that I'm doing. In some ways that might be knowing better what to ask of an LLM, in other ways it means having a mental model that I can call on to diagnose bugs quickly. 

In the end, though, I'm trying to make certain procedures, design patterns, conventions, whatever, more permanent in my brain so I can build on that knowledge. I think that being able to think and reason about problems is worth it even at the expense of feeling uncomfortable not knowing. I love it when the senior engineers I work with say 'I don't know' to something. Yes, we're all humans. And that's the thing, we're so stubborn in our need to search or answers and figure stuff out.
